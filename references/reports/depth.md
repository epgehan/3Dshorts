# Depth, staging, occlusion and selective focus

## Evidence and limits

Reviewed all seven overview sheets, then 38 native extracted frames with within-shot comparisons. Sources are 360×640 at 30 fps. Frame numbering below is zero based: timestamp = frame / 30. Names are private study aliases only. Do not ship the study images or reproduce their scenes. This report describes visible projection, overlap and sharpness; it does not recover geometry, focal length, camera tracks, physical aperture, or a particular engine. A drawn image that stays broad to camera is consistent with a billboard, but is not proof of one. JPEG/compression, texture filtering, motion blur, focus blur and authored softness can coexist at this resolution.

Priors consulted: the old cartoon-3d-short SKILL.md and the supplied DESIGN_GUIDE.md. Their general flat-figure/dimensional-set principle survives. Their numeric lens values, fog distances, mandatory camera creep and foreground frequency are production history, not measurements of these seven uploads. Do not carry forward the old claim that precisely one third of source screen time uses DOF: this study has not measured that prevalence. Newly confirmed subtitle policy is ALL CAPS Arial, white fill with black outline; source sentence-case examples are evidence of placement/occlusion only and do not override this policy.

## Main finding

Depth comes first from perspective-built environments, scale relationships, overlapping planes and ground contact. Shallow focus is a selective extra. The figures preserve flat drawn faces and thin graphic limbs while cars, counters, chairs, forest trunks, coasters, rails and buildings expose converging edges, visible side/top planes, repeated units and mutual occlusion. Some shots use almost stage-like frontal layering; others travel through deep sets or around large machinery. Both belong to the observed language.

The useful production rule is to build enough dimensional set to support the shot, then place a readable illustrated figure in it. Avoid using blur to compensate for absent spatial construction. Conversely, do not model a round doll because the world is dimensional.

## Flat figures, turns and dimensional props

Do not reduce the evidence to “every character is a permanently camera-facing plane.” The drawings retain nearly frontal readable faces across many oblique sets: mirror f270–300, spot f720–780 and the driver framed by a dimensional cabin in sample-1 f0–30/f1380–1395. These are compatible with oriented artwork/cards and pose changes. Their exact representation is unknown.

There is affirmative front/back treatment in cashiers: f210 shows the customer's face; f270 shows a full brown back-of-head silhouette and rear yellow torso; f300 continues the departure through the door. The back view in knight f900 also stages the priest facing toward the distant knight. Use alternate front/three-quarter/back drawings where the story demands a turn; do not rotate a thin front drawing until it vanishes edge-on, and do not keep facial features painted onto the back of the head. This is a proposed rendering solution to the visible requirement, not a source-engine claim.

A held object, vehicle, armor, horse or chest can have more shading/volume than a face. Occlusion hierarchy matters more than enforcing one asset technique: hand over document; torso behind counter; chair armrest in front of seated pelvis; door pillar in front of driver; suspension straps connecting chest to pole. Small costume gradients or armor highlights do not license volumetric skin, sculpted noses or tubular limbs.

## Practical staging controls (proposals, not source measurements)

1. Plan three named planes for each normal shot: a near framing/contact layer, the acting plane, and a readable setting layer. Add far scenery when geography or scale needs it. A clean close-up may legitimately have only subject plus softened rear set.
2. Establish floor/track/desk perspective before adding a character. Repeated tiles, floorboards, sleepers, shelves and rail ties must diminish with distance and converge coherently. Avoid unrelated vanishing directions within one set.
3. Anchor feet to the same floor used by furniture. Add a compact foot-contact shadow plus an appropriately directed cast shadow when lighting warrants it. Seated poses need pelvis/seat contact and consistent thigh/leg emergence. Suspended objects need visible supports; do not fake a hanging chest as a floor prop with a detached shadow.
4. Stage a silhouette read at 360×640 as well as delivery resolution. Eyes, hand action and causal contact should stay legible. Use a small figure when comparison with a huge structure is the point (shape f750), rather than making every person dominate frame.
5. Put foreground objects where they have a role: shoulder for dialogue, sill/window for interior framing, chair for occupancy, beam/rail for structure, vehicle dashboard for enclosure. Cropping is acceptable if the object is recognizable. Avoid arbitrary huge edge blobs.
6. Preserve correct depth writing/occlusion for transparent-cutout characters. Sort limbs/held props intentionally, avoid halos around alpha contours, and test cards crossing furniture. If a single card cannot represent a hand in front of a prop while torso is behind it, split only the necessary artwork into articulated layers.
7. Test every moving shot at start/middle/end and at crossings. A rail that initially clears a face may cover it later; a near beam can fill the image after a modest camera translation. Do not solve every obstruction by removing world objects: choose sight lines and preserve useful foreground framing.
8. Use scale deliberately: foreground crop for intimacy; near/mid/far size progression for a corridor; small figure/large repeated structure for comparison. Relative visual size is measured in the frame, not inferred in meters.

## Practical focus controls (proposals, not measured lens settings)

- Choose the narrative target first: speaking face, hand/tool contact, rear knight, or whole loop. Focus can be behind foreground people (knight f900; cashiers f210). “Nearest eyes” is not a universal rule.
- Start wides, scale explanations, travel geography and repeated-pattern demonstrations with broad sharpness. Introduce blur only when it strengthens hierarchy without deleting necessary evidence.
- For reaction close-ups, soften the rear set visibly while preserving face outline, eyes and costume contour. For over-shoulder dialogue, near shoulder can be more blurred than the focused speaker. Ordinary frontal medium shots can retain clear shelves, window streets and counter geometry.
- If using a depth-aware postprocess, apply it to both environment and artwork in a consistent depth order. Composite captions afterward so they stay crisp. Alpha cards need valid depth behavior; an invisible rectangle must not blur or occlude the scene as a solid card.
- Blur radii must scale with output resolution. No source-measured radius is claimed here. The previous bridge value of 10–16 px at 1080-wide corresponds to roughly 3.3–5.3 px at native 360 width and is only a starting experiment, not a target established by these clips. Judge native-size edge transitions and face/background hierarchy before choosing any numerical preset.
- Avoid blurry borders around otherwise sharp hair, ink, rails and chairs; excessive depth discontinuity leakage looks like cutout compositing. Preserve rear edges visible through spaces between arms and legs.
- Distinguish vignette, localized lamp glow, low-resolution distance detail, motion smearing and optical inserts from DOF. Do not apply a universal haze/blur layer because one dark shot looks soft.

## Acceptance and failure checks

Pass when the actor reads as an illustration embedded in a coherent set; the floor/seat/hand contacts are explicit; overlap order supports the action; wide shots communicate geography; focus follows attention; and the face remains readable under lighting/glass/tints.

Reject: spherical 3D heads replacing drawn faces; paper characters floating above ground; detached contact shadows; arms disappearing behind the wrong counter edge; back-facing figures retaining frontal faces; foreground objects hiding the causal gesture; uniformly blurred worlds; all shots sharp with no selective-focus option; all shots blurred by default; copied source staging; and claims of recovered camera/lens/geometry based on these compressed projections.

## Revisions to inherit into the refined skill

Keep flat illustrated actors inside dimensional sets as the core requirement. Make front/back artwork and necessary layer splitting explicit. Replace “focus on eyes of front row” with “focus on the narrative acting plane; near framing figures may be soft.” Treat selective focus and foreground framing as shot choices, not fixed quotas. Retain contact shadows, perspective repetitions and occlusion as hard QA checks. Keep old bridge lens/fog numbers labeled as implementation examples only. Use source-backed evidence IDs above when refining new scene presets.
