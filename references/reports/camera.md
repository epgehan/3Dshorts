# Camera, framing and perspective — seven-video moving-reference study

## Evidence and limits

Reviewed all seven `overview.jpg` files, all seven `cuts-00.jpg` files, and native consecutive-frame sheets: mirror f1440–1499; knight f120–179; spot f180–239; cashiers f0–59 and f1320–1379; shape f1200–1259 and f1320–1379; railroads f120–179 and f600–659; sample-1 f1440–1559. Source is 360×640, 30 fps; time = frame / 30. The overview is a three-second survey, not a shot-boundary ledger. Named overview frames below establish that a composition exists, not its exact beginning or end. Candidate transition detection misses many dissolves and must not become a claimed shot count.

The affine CSV is an image-feature estimate at 180×320 excluding the lower 28%; moving characters, sparse features, depth and dissolves contaminate it. It is not camera tracking. No focal length, world distance, camera path in meters, renderer, physical lens or true roll angle is recoverable as fact from these images alone. Old skill camera numbers are implementation history, not measurements of these seven videos.

## Main finding

The camera behaves like an active narrator. It changes what information the viewer receives: an ordinary exchange becomes an extreme insert, a small character becomes a scale marker under an enormous structure, a window becomes an entry route into a room, and an apparent poster becomes a whole wall of repeated evidence. Strong perspective, location-specific framing and rapid reframing are more important than a universal slow zoom. Motion has clear targets; quieter intervals retain face and gesture readability.

The new footage supports a wider vocabulary than the old skill's 18–40-frame glide plus 4–5° roll. It includes restrained sideways drift, rapid continuous close-in moves, large pullbacks, elevated scale views, vehicle interiors, lateral reveal moves, foreground wipes, dissolves, and sustained strongly tilted compositions. Do not force every shot into the same easing, roll or push-in rate.

## Framing and angles: observations

### Height and pitch are story controls

Most exchanges preserve readable faces with a frontal or gently oblique view. Environments remain dimensional: counters, tiled floors, benches, track beds and facades converge around the flat figures. The camera is not locked to eye height. Shape f1200–1239 looks steeply down the track from a high ride position; the front red vehicle/platform edge is visible at the bottom early in that interval, then leaves as the track dominates. This is an elevated ride view, not a demonstrated perfectly vertical overhead. Knight f750 looks over small buildings toward a dense tree mass. Railroads f120–179 uses a high/wide chasm view to expose the gap, supports and construction scale.

Conversely, shape f1245–1327 places the car and roadside signs against a large sky from a low roadway view; shape f1650 makes the loop climb high over the viewer. Railroads f0–30 uses rails rushing toward a narrow vanishing point with canyon walls towering above. Sample-1 f1380–1490 is explicitly an interior windshield POV: roof, dashboard, wheel and hood form a deep frame around the external car. Match these functions rather than attaching “low angle” to every heroic character shot.

### Dutch framing exists, but the road is not the horizon

Sample-1 f0 and f1504–1559 visibly incline the interior roof, seat, dashboard and character arrangement. The tilted frame is sustained while action continues; it is not merely a one-frame impact shake. Mirror f1470–1499 has strongly oblique window jambs/board geometry during a room-entry move. Shape f1333–1379 contains a flipping car; its rotation alone must not be mislabeled camera roll. Road banking, convergence and diagonal tracks can also create diagonals without an inclined camera.

Recommended control: expose shot-level roll as an independent creative key, with a level horizon reference/debug grid. Approve angle from a still and then from motion. Do not inherit 4–5° as an enforced reference-wide range. The exact absolute tilt in these shots has not been fitted against known verticals.

### Foreground is functional and sometimes fills the picture

Knight f145–154: a very near tree trunk sweeps across and hides the village while revealing a waterfront on its other side; f173–179 a near ladder begins a second wipe. This is much stronger than a decorative plant at a corner. Mirror f1464–1499 uses the shop window/jamb and near betting board as a traversed foreground layer. Sample-1 f480 shows leaves across most of the frame; f493 reveals the red car beyond. Cashiers f1320–1335 lets an extremely close dark pointer/arm cross over a small poster before pulling back. Railroads f930 frames the face through a carriage opening; sample-1 f1290 frames a driver through the side window.

Recommendations: storyboard each foreground object's entry, maximum coverage, exit and resulting reveal. It can briefly obscure the subject when that occlusion is the transition, but should not accidentally cover the face during an explanation. Use recognizable silhouettes and consistent occlusion. A near object should travel faster than the far set during translation; a flat edge overlay should not imitate a real doorway unless intentionally graphic.

### Perceived perspective and focus

Sample-1 f1504–1559 gives a conspicuously expanded interior: nearby seat/dashboard/roof planes spread strongly while figures fit inside the cabin. Mirror f1470–1499 makes the near board enlarge and sweep relative to rear furniture; spot f210–239 combines converging ground tiles, glass facade and small figures. These read as broad perspective; no numeric FOV or focal length is claimed. Long track/loop views use repeated geometry and diminishing scale to explain distance. Face close-ups keep the face dominant and simplify or soften rear scenery; wide geography retains enough set detail to explain location. No exact optical focus distance or physically correct DOF is established here. Use shallow focus selectively, and check that a blur pass does not flatten the depth cues that the camera is revealing.

## Translation, zoom and 2D pan: what can and cannot be claimed

A uniform enlargement can be made by a lens change, camera translation toward a nearly planar subject, or 2D scaling. An affine scale alone cannot distinguish these. Cashiers poster pullback is dominated by a wall plane: a 2D animated pullback could reproduce much of it. Do not insist that it proves a physical dolly. Mirror room entry is stronger evidence for scene-relative translation because near window/board shapes sweep and reveal rear room surfaces at different rates; still, it could be created with layered compositing. Knight's foreground wipe is clear layered depth behavior, but does not uniquely identify its authoring engine.

Recommended implementation: use a real perspective camera and depth-separated layers when the shot requires newly revealed surfaces, occlusion or different near/far velocities; use a 2D reframing move for deliberate inserts/graphic boards if it produces the intended result. Verify with foreground and rear landmark trajectories, not camera-key values alone. Preserve the flat cartoon face while changing the set perspective.

## Edits and transition timing observed directly

- Cashiers f30–39 is still a counter/character composition; f40–44 rapidly enlarges the clerk and redirects down to the ticket; f50–59 is an extreme tabletop insert. This is a continuous information move in the inspected burst, not a reason to replace it with a generic cut.
- Spot f193–204 blends the number-grid image with the glass facade. The old and new scenes coexist in intervening frames; then the facade approach continues. It is a dissolve plus a camera-like move, not one affine camera path through both places.
- Mirror f1442–1448 blends judge-booth geometry into an exterior street, then f1464 onward passes into the shop view. Keep edit opacity separate from camera interpolation.
- Shape f1328–1333 visibly blends frontal car/road into a trailing-road view; f1345 onward the car starts rolling. Do not classify the changing car orientation as an impact camera shake.
- Sample-1 f1495–1505 blends dashboard POV into the tilted interior view. The camera viewpoint changes significantly even though the car remains the story location.
- Knight f145–154 uses actual foreground occlusion as the location transition, and f173 onward starts another wipe. Such wipes can mask scene swaps without a black frame or hard discontinuity.

These inspected dissolves last roughly several to a dozen frames; boundaries are visually estimated, not exact opacity-key measurements. There is no evidence that every transition should last 18–40 frames or that every large viewpoint change is a hard cut.

## Recommended production contract

1. Give each shot an explicit camera job: establish geography, expose scale, follow action, reveal information, inspect an object, or read an expression. Name the foreground/midground/background landmarks.
2. Store position, target, roll, FOV/perceived-perspective setting, focus target and transition independently. Store screen-space acceptance goals as well: subject size, headroom, safe face region, near-object coverage and end-frame information.
3. Use at least a full figure/context view, a medium exchange, an object insert, a face close-up, and a wide scale shot where the story needs them. These are vocabulary options, not a required mechanical five-shot cycle.
4. Block the important end frames first. Test midpoint and every adjacent frame through fast moves, occlusion wipes and tilted close shots. Ensure billboard facing does not visibly snap or detach feet from the floor when the camera crosses.
5. Tune duration from narration and visual travel. Slow drift can support dry dialogue; a rapid push/downward redirect can make an object the punchline; a pullback can disclose an unexpectedly large set. Avoid forcing all holds to visibly enlarge when actor or environmental motion already carries the shot.
6. Validate readable faces, hand/prop contacts, clean rail/counter occlusion, actual perspective depth and a clear reveal. A high motion metric is neither a quality target nor proof that a static-looking shot needs more movement.
7. Keep caption space in the framing plan. Current user preference overrides the old no-captions rule: ALL CAPS Arial, white fill, black outline only. Do not choose a dark backplate as a substitute for protecting the visible action.

## Changes needed to the old camera guidance

Retain purposeful smooth glides, camera-facing illustrated characters, scene-aware foreground and fast impact accents as available tools. Remove universal assertions that every hold creeps, all large changes cut, every roll is 4–5°, all glides take 18–40 frames, and image flow measures physical camera speed. Replace these with shot-dependent controls and screen-space verification. Foreground wipes, vehicle POVs, elevated ride/scale views, large pullback reveals, broad-perspective interiors and short dissolves are supported directly by the new moving footage and should be first-class choices.
